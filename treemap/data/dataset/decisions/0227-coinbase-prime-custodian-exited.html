Explanation

Coinbase held these 930 withdrawal addresses in custody for Coinbase Prime clients. The decision records custody only: who chose and ran the staking is settled elsewhere, and where nothing else shows an allocator Coinbase is assumed to be it. The validators, 34,747 in all, were deposited through Coinbase's batch deposit contract from January 2022; they held as much as 735k ETH in January 2025, and nearly all have since exited.

The custody test. Every one of the 930 addresses has a depositing account to which Coinbase's custody gas account 0xa863…6c0f sent a transaction. That account pays small amounts of gas to accounts whose keys Coinbase holds: it paid for 361 of the 363 deposit transactions of Grayscale's ether funds, whose fund pages (ETHE, ETH) name Coinbase Custody as custodian. Before 2025 the gas often arrived just after an account was funded rather than just before it deposited, so the test looks at any time rather than a short window before the first deposit. For 542 of the addresses the depositing account was also first funded by one of Coinbase's two custody wallets. Custody accounts also pass ETH along among themselves: one received 72,592 ETH, was given gas by 0xa863…6c0f nineteen blocks later, and then funded seventeen of the depositing accounts.

Why custody is recorded apart from allocation. Coinbase Prime clients choose their staking providers, as Figment's announcement with Coinbase Prime describes. Most of these validators never signed graffiti and share no fee recipient with Coinbase's fleet, so decision 0226 does not cover them. Validators on these addresses that another claim gives to a provider keep it (for example those paying Coinbase's own fee recipient, or signing Galaxy's graffiti).

Wallets into which Coinbase's retail staking pays out are not used as a test: they also fund ordinary customers' withdrawals. The decision would change with evidence that an address is held by its owner, or by another custodian, rather than by Coinbase for a Prime client.
